# Pleasure, Suffering, and Balance
## A Research Program by Ori Cohen

**Author:** Ori Cohen (אורי כהן)  
**First formulation:** 2026-09-19  
**Project:** IMA — אמא  
**Status:** Research framework / philosophical hypothesis

## Abstract

This paper proposes a research program centered on a question:

If human traditions have developed extensive analyses of suffering, can pleasure, joy, vitality, connection, creativity, meaning, and flourishing be investigated with comparable conceptual depth?

A second question follows:

> What principles might allow a person to experience both suffering and pleasure without being governed by either?

This paper does not claim that pleasure has never been studied. Epicurean philosophy, Aristotelian eudaimonia, utilitarian and hedonist traditions, and modern research on hedonic and eudaimonic well-being provide substantial precedents.

The proposed contribution is therefore a synthesis and research program rather than a claim of historical uniqueness:

1. systematically map pleasure as an object of inquiry;
2. compare its conditions and dynamics with those of suffering;
3. investigate a third domain of balance in which neither avoidance of suffering nor pursuit of pleasure becomes the sole organizing principle of life.

## 1. The Question

A central Buddhist framework investigates suffering, its conditions, cessation, and a path of practice.

Buddhist traditions also contain substantial analysis of pleasure, attachment, impermanence, and liberation. This research therefore does not claim that Buddhism ignored pleasure.

Instead it asks:

> Can pleasure be investigated with a depth comparable to the philosophical investigation of suffering, and can the relationship between the two yield a distinct theory of balance?

## 2. A Four-Part Inquiry of Pleasure

### 2.1 Pleasure exists

Pleasure includes sensory enjoyment but may also include:

- joy
- beauty
- love
- curiosity
- play
- music
- creativity
- discovery
- connection
- vitality
- meaningful engagement

### 2.2 Pleasure has conditions

Candidate conditions include:

- safety
- autonomy
- connection
- embodiment
- attention
- competence
- novelty
- love
- play
- meaning

These should be treated as hypotheses to be tested against existing evidence rather than as established universal laws.

### 2.3 Pleasures differ

Immediate pleasantness and longer-term flourishing need not be identical.

Research should distinguish forms of pleasure by:

- duration
- consequences
- dependence
- relational effects
- agency
- compatibility with other dimensions of well-being

### 2.4 Capacity for pleasure may itself be cultivable

The inquiry concerns not only obtaining pleasant experiences, but the capacity to:

- notice
- receive
- create
- share
- regulate
- sustain

positive experience.

## 3. The Balance Hypothesis

The proposed third domain is neither a numerical midpoint between suffering and pleasure nor a simple mixture of the two.

Possible paired dimensions include:

| Pole A | Pole B |
|---|---|
| suffering | pleasure |
| avoidance | participation |
| release | creation |
| acceptance | desire |
| stillness | enthusiasm |
| non-attachment | love |
| awareness | experience |
| meaning | enjoyment |

The central hypothesis is:

> Mature flourishing may involve the capacity to remain consciously engaged with life while neither suffering nor pleasure becomes the sole ruler of action.

This is a hypothesis for investigation, not a conclusion.

## 4. Prior Intellectual Territory

The literature already contains substantial work relevant to this program.

Ryan and Deci distinguished hedonic well-being, centered on pleasure attainment and pain avoidance, from eudaimonic well-being, centered on meaning, self-realization, and functioning.

Later empirical research continued investigating whether hedonic and eudaimonic orientations are distinct, complementary, or overlapping dimensions of well-being.

Buddhist traditions provide another important comparison because they analyze pleasant experience together with attachment, impermanence, suffering, and liberation.

Therefore this project does not claim:

> "No one has ever studied pleasure."

The defensible research claim is narrower:

> This project proposes a particular synthesis in which pleasure, suffering, and their possible balancing principles are treated as three connected objects of inquiry.

## 5. Research Questions

1. What distinct kinds of pleasure can be identified?
2. What conditions increase or decrease the capacity to experience pleasure?
3. Which pleasures produce short-term enjoyment but longer-term costs?
4. Which forms of pleasure correlate with autonomy, connection, meaning, vitality, or flourishing?
5. How does attachment alter the relationship between pleasure and suffering?
6. Can pleasure support the capacity to face suffering without becoming avoidance?
7. Can acceptance of suffering coexist with active cultivation of pleasure?
8. What would constitute a genuine theory of balance rather than a compromise between two extremes?
9. How do these questions differ across cultures, philosophies, and individual lives?
10. Which claims can be empirically tested, and which remain philosophical interpretations?
