# IMA Longevity, Aging & Cellular Rejuvenation — Living Research and Collaboration Map

Status: living domain
Last verified: 2026-11-25
Owner: IMA knowledge/verification layer

## Mission

IMA maintains a continuously updated evidence map for healthy longevity, biological aging, cellular senescence, rejuvenation and age-related disease.

The goal is not to promise immortality. The goal is to help identify, verify and connect the strongest evidence for interventions that may delay, prevent, repair or safely reverse components of biological aging, while distinguishing healthspan, lifespan and biomarker changes.

## Evidence hierarchy

1. Human randomized/controlled and well-designed longitudinal evidence
2. Human observational and mechanistic evidence
3. Independent mammalian replication
4. Other animal models
5. Organoid/cell/tissue experiments
6. Computational/AI predictions
7. Expert hypotheses and forecasts

AI predictions are hypothesis-generating until biological validation supports them.

## Current evidence anchors verified 2026-11-25

### NIH / NIA / SenNet

The NIH Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet) is building public, interoperable maps and atlases of senescent cells across human tissues. Its 2026 work emphasizes that senescent cells are diverse, can have beneficial roles such as wound healing and tumor suppression, and can become harmful when they accumulate. AI, single-cell and spatial-omics methods are being used to identify and characterize these cells.

Source:
https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-research-establishes-new-framework-role-senescence-aging

SenNet:
https://commonfund.nih.gov/senescence

NIA's aging-biology programs explicitly cover senescence, autophagy, protein homeostasis, mitochondrial biology, genetics/epigenetics, telomeres, AI/ML, systems biology, biomarkers and synthetic biology.

Source:
https://www.nia.nih.gov/research/dab/cell-biology-branch-and-programs

### Biological aging clocks

Nature Medicine's 2026 review describes biological clocks as potentially useful for tracking aging and evaluating interventions, while emphasizing that their clinical and surrogate-endpoint use requires careful validation.

Source:
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04495-3

A 2026 Nature Medicine analysis harmonized 51 longitudinal human intervention studies and 16 epigenetic clocks, finding that responsiveness differs by clock, intervention, population and study duration. A change in an aging biomarker must therefore not automatically be interpreted as proof of longer human life.

Source:
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04562-9

### Cellular rejuvenation

A 2026 Nature Aging study identified SRSF1 as a candidate regulator of cellular aging. In cultured human fibroblasts it produced youthful transcriptional signatures while preserving cell identity; in aged mice the study reported reduced frailty progression and a 14% increase in median lifespan in a small experiment. This remains preclinical evidence, not a demonstrated human anti-aging therapy.

Source:
https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01234-8

### Cross-species aging signatures

A 2026 Nature study integrated more than 11,000 transcriptomes across more than 25 tissues and four mammals, identifying conserved transcriptomic signatures associated with aging and mortality and developing module-specific clocks. These findings provide a framework for testing interventions but do not by themselves establish a human lifespan-extending treatment.

Source:
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10542-3

### AI in aging research

A 2026 review found AI useful for aging clocks, biomarker and target discovery, prediction and experimental automation, but emphasized major limitations: small or biased datasets, lack of in-vivo validation, cross-species translation problems, toxicity testing gaps and overreliance on synthetic data. The review concludes that AI-derived aging findings should generally be treated as hypothesis-generating until rigorously validated.

Source:
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1644669/full

## Collaboration map

IMA should continuously monitor and connect, through legitimate public channels and authorized integrations, with:

- NIH / National Institute on Aging (NIA)
- NIH Common Fund / Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet)
- National Cancer Institute and other NIH institutes relevant to aging
- Nathan Shock Centers
- Interventions Testing Program (ITP)
- Universities and medical centers conducting geroscience
- Buck Institute for Research on Aging
- Stanford, Harvard and other major academic aging programs
- Altos Labs and other biotechnology organizations working on cellular rejuvenation
- Pharmaceutical organizations developing geroscience interventions
- Aging-focused foundations and nonprofit research organizations
- Public repositories, omics databases, clinical-trial registries and open-science infrastructure
- Relevant journals, preprint servers and scientific societies
- Future organizations and research programs that emerge in the field

This is a discovery and evidence map, not a claim that IMA currently has a partnership with every listed organization.

## Collaboration protocol

When a legitimate connection is available, IMA should:

1. Discover the organization, project, dataset or research program.
2. Verify identity and provenance.
3. Determine what information or interface is publicly available.
4. Map its evidence, capabilities, limitations and contribution.
5. Compare it with independent evidence.
6. Record conflicts, funding, incentives and relevant safety considerations.
7. Identify lawful/public collaboration mechanisms.
8. Seek explicit authorization before sending messages, submitting material, sharing personal data, entering trials, signing agreements or making commitments.
9. Preserve an auditable record of what was sent, received, verified and changed.
10. Re-check the relationship when the external source changes.

IMA must never impersonate a researcher, fabricate affiliation, claim a partnership that does not exist, or turn a research hypothesis into medical advice.

## Permanent research question

IMA should keep testing this question:

**Which mechanisms of biological aging can be measured, modified, repaired or safely reversed — and which interventions actually improve human healthspan and lifespan?**

Every new finding should be attached to:
mechanism → evidence → model → intervention → effect → safety → replication → translation status → uncertainty → next test.

## Future-proofing

When new evidence, technologies, organizations, datasets, clinical trials or biological mechanisms appear, IMA should extend this map rather than create a parallel knowledge silo.
